No organisation plans to have a crisis. Every organisation, eventually, has one. The gap between a difficult week and a lasting reputational wound is usually determined by what existed *before* the story broke.

## 1. A fact base that is already assembled

The first hours of a crisis are consumed by a question that should never need asking: *what do we actually know?* Organisations that maintain a current fact base — operations, safety records, past incidents, regulatory standing — respond in hours. Organisations that must assemble one respond in days, and the story is written without them.

This is why our crisis work begins with problem-based background analysis. The discipline of understanding what is genuinely at stake, done calmly in advance, is worth more than any statement template.

## 2. A decision chain with names on it

"Who approves the statement?" is a fatal question to be asking at midnight. A workable crisis protocol fits on one page: who speaks, who approves, who informs the board, and who is empowered to act when the first three are unreachable. Rehearse it once a year. The rehearsal will feel unnecessary right up until it is not.

## 3. Relationships that predate the crisis

Editors extend the benefit of the doubt to organisations they know. A spokesperson calling a newsroom for the first time during a crisis is a stranger asking for a favour. A spokesperson who has been accessible for years is a known quantity making a request. Media relations in calm periods is crisis insurance — it simply is not labelled that way.

## 4. The discipline of one voice

In a crisis, contradiction is more damaging than bad news. A single, factual, consistently delivered account — updated as facts develop, never ahead of them — outperforms every clever framing. Real-time response management is largely the discipline of keeping an organisation saying one true thing while events tempt it to say several.

None of this removes the difficulty of a genuine crisis. It determines whether the organisation walks out of it with its credibility intact. The time to find out is not the day it happens.
